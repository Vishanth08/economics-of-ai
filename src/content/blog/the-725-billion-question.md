In 2025, the four largest US hyperscalers spent a combined $410 billion on capital expenditure — already a record. In 2026, that number is on track to hit $725 billion. That is not a typo. It is a 77% increase, in a single year, from companies that were already spending at the upper limit of what most analysts considered sustainable.

To put that in perspective: $725 billion is larger than the GDP of Switzerland. It is larger than the entire global semiconductor industry's annual revenue just a few years ago. And it is being spent by four companies, in twelve months, almost entirely on one thing — the infrastructure to run artificial intelligence at scale.

This is the story everyone in tech already half-knows. What is less understood is *where exactly* this money goes, why the number keeps moving up instead of down, and what has to be true for this bet to pay off.

## The breakdown, company by company

Amazon is the largest single spender in 2026, guiding to roughly $200 billion in capital expenditure. Alphabet sits close behind at $175–185 billion. Microsoft has set calendar-year guidance at $190 billion — notably higher than the $152 billion analysts had expected just months earlier. Meta, despite having no public cloud business to monetize directly, is still guiding to $115–135 billion.

What's striking is not just the size of each number, but how often they keep revising upward mid-year. Microsoft's own CFO, Amy Hood, attributed part of the increase to rising memory chip and component costs, and told investors the company expects to remain capacity-constrained well into the year — meaning Azure is turning away AI workloads it physically cannot serve yet, even at $190 billion of spend.

That detail matters more than the headline figure. These companies are not overbuilding speculatively. They are underbuilding relative to demand, and racing to close the gap.

## What the money actually buys

Capex headlines tend to collapse everything into one bucket — "AI infrastructure" — but it is worth pulling apart what that actually means, because each piece has a different economic logic:

**GPUs and AI accelerators.** The single largest line item. Nvidia's data center GPUs remain the default choice, though Google (TPUs), Amazon (Trainium), and Microsoft (Maia) are all pushing custom silicon to reduce dependency and cost-per-token over time. This is the most depreciation-sensitive piece of the spend — GPU useful life is typically modeled at 3–6 years, and faster-than-expected hardware obsolescence is one of the biggest hidden risks in these capex numbers.

**Data center construction.** Physical buildings, often built at a scale that did not exist five years ago. Meta's "Hyperion" campus in Louisiana is designed to draw up to 5 gigawatts of power at peak — roughly equivalent to the electricity consumption of 4.2 million homes. This is not incremental data center growth; it is industrial-scale construction with multi-decade depreciation schedules.

**Power and cooling infrastructure.** Increasingly the actual bottleneck, not chip supply. Securing enough electricity — and increasingly, building dedicated power generation — has become a strategic priority alongside GPU procurement. This is also where the spending starts to intersect with public infrastructure, utility regulation, and local opposition.

**Networking.** The unglamorous but essential layer connecting thousands of GPUs into coherent training clusters. Bandwidth between chips, not just raw chip count, increasingly determines effective compute output.

## The uncomfortable financial reality

Here is where the analysis gets less triumphant. This spending is showing up directly in free cash flow, and the market has so far tolerated it — but conditionally.

Microsoft's capex-to-operating-cash-flow ratio has climbed past 50%. Meta's free cash flow has compressed as capex has outrun operating cash generation. These are not small companies absorbing a rounding error; this is structural margin compression at firms that, until recently, were prized specifically for capital-light, high-margin software economics.

The bull case — and it is a real one, not just hype — is that cloud and AI revenue growth is still accelerating fast enough to justify it. Google's cloud revenue, for instance, jumped 63% year-over-year in a recent quarter, and one analyst covering the space bluntly called the bear thesis "garbage" in response. Demand, on the numbers available so far, looks real.

The bear case is just as straightforward: the moment AI-driven revenue growth decelerates while capex keeps climbing — which it is currently doing, not slowing — multiple compression could be severe. Wall Street has priced these stocks partly on the assumption that this spending converts efficiently into durable revenue. If that conversion rate disappoints even moderately, the math gets uncomfortable fast, because the capex is not easily reversible. You cannot un-build a data center.

## The asymmetry that explains the behavior

The simplest explanation for why four competing companies are all spending at once, despite the financial strain, is this: each fears being structurally short on compute more than it fears overspending.

This is a classic case of payoff asymmetry. If you underinvest and AI demand keeps accelerating, you permanently cede market share to a competitor who built ahead of demand — a mistake that, in cloud computing, can take years to claw back. If you overinvest and demand plateaus, you take a margin hit, but you survive, and you can slow capex growth the following year.

Given that asymmetry, overspending is the individually rational choice for each company — even though, collectively, it produces an industry-wide capex level that none of them would have chosen as efficient in isolation. This is worth remembering whenever a headline frames the spending as irrational exuberance. From the seat of any one CFO, it is closer to a defensive necessity.

## What to watch next

A few signals will matter more than the headline capex figures themselves over the next few quarters:

- **Cloud and AI revenue growth rates** at each company, relative to capex growth rates. The gap between the two is the real story.
- **Depreciation schedule changes.** If any hyperscaler quietly extends the useful life assumed for GPUs, it is a signal they are trying to soften the earnings impact of this spend — worth watching closely.
- **Power procurement deals**, including any moves toward dedicated generation (nuclear, gas turbines) rather than grid purchases, since this increasingly gates how fast capacity can actually come online.
- **Whether smaller hyperscalers and neoclouds** (CoreWeave, Oracle Cloud, and similar) can keep raising capital to compete, or whether capex intensity becomes a moat that only the largest four can sustain.

$725 billion is not a peak. On current guidance, it is closer to a midpoint of a multi-year cycle — one analysis puts aggregate hyperscaler capex at $7.6 trillion cumulatively between 2026 and 2031. Whether that turns out to be the soundest infrastructure bet of the decade, or the most expensive overcorrection in tech history, will not be visible in this year's numbers. It will show up two or three years from now, in the gap between what was built and what was actually used.
